## 1. MOAT (DURABLE COMPETITIVE ADVANTAGE)

### 1.1 What a moat is
`tags: moat, quality, competitive-advantage`
A "moat" is a durable competitive advantage that protects a business's profits from
competitors the way a moat protects a castle. Buffett looks for businesses whose moats
are widening over time, not shrinking. Common moat sources: low-cost production, a
trusted brand, network effects, high switching costs, and regulatory or scale barriers.
The key test is durability: will this advantage still exist in 10–20 years? A wonderful
business is one a competitor with unlimited money still could not easily displace.

### 1.2 Wonderful business at a fair price
`tags: moat, quality, price, philosophy`
Buffett's evolution (influenced by Munger) was from buying "cheap, mediocre businesses"
to buying "wonderful businesses at a fair price." A wonderful business earns high returns
on capital and can reinvest at those high returns for years. Paying a fair price for such
a business beats paying a cheap price for a declining one, because the wonderful business
keeps compounding while the cheap one keeps deteriorating. Quality first, then price.

## 2. MARGIN OF SAFETY

### 2.1 Margin of safety principle
`tags: margin-of-safety, valuation, risk, core`
Margin of safety means buying a business for meaningfully less than its estimated
intrinsic value, so that even if your estimate is somewhat wrong, you are protected from
permanent loss. Borrowed from Benjamin Graham: if you think a bridge holds 30 tons, only
drive 10-ton trucks across it. In practice, compare current price to a conservative
intrinsic value; the bigger the discount, the larger the safety cushion. Price paid
determines return — the same business is a good or bad investment depending on price.

### 2.2 Intrinsic value
`tags: intrinsic-value, valuation, DCF, core`
Intrinsic value is the discounted value of the cash a business will produce over its
remaining life. It cannot be calculated precisely — it is a reasoned estimate, not a
single exact number, so a range is more honest than a point figure. Two people will get
different intrinsic values from the same facts because of different assumptions about
future cash. This is why Buffett prefers simple, predictable businesses: their future
cash is easier to estimate, so the intrinsic-value estimate is more trustworthy.

## 3. OWNER EARNINGS

### 3.1 Owner earnings definition
`tags: owner-earnings, methodology, cash-flow, core`
Owner earnings (Buffett's 1986 definition) is the cash an owner could take out of a
business each year without harming its competitive position. Roughly: net income, plus
depreciation and amortization and other non-cash charges, minus the maintenance capital
expenditure needed to keep the business competitive. It is usually a better measure of
real earning power than reported accounting profit, which can be distorted by non-cash
items. Reported earnings answer "what do the rules say we earned"; owner earnings answer
"how much cash can the owner actually keep."

### 3.2 Maintenance vs growth capex
`tags: owner-earnings, capex, methodology`
A subtle but important point in owner-earnings analysis: only *maintenance* capex (the
spending required to keep current earning power) should be subtracted. *Growth* capex
(new factories, new capacity) is really an investment in future earnings, not a cost of
running today's business. Standard "free cash flow" subtracts all capex and therefore
understates the earning power of a company investing heavily to grow. Estimating the
maintenance/growth split is one of the most judgment-dependent steps in valuation.

## 4. TEMPERAMENT & MR. MARKET

### 4.1 Mr. Market
`tags: behavior, mr-market, psychology, core`
Graham's "Mr. Market" is an imaginary business partner who shows up every day offering to
buy or sell at a price driven by his mood — sometimes euphoric and overpriced, sometimes
depressed and far too cheap. The lesson: the market is there to serve you, not to instruct
you. You are free to ignore his quotes and act only when his price is clearly attractive.
A falling price on a business whose fundamentals are unchanged is Mr. Market in a gloomy
mood — an opportunity to buy, not a reason to panic.

### 4.2 Be fearful when others are greedy
`tags: behavior, contrarian, psychology`
"Be fearful when others are greedy, and greedy when others are fearful." Buffett argues
the biggest investing edge is temperament, not intelligence — the ability to stay rational
when others are euphoric or panicking. Market crashes transfer wealth from the impatient
to the patient. The investor who keeps buying good businesses during a crash, when prices
are low and fear is high, tends to do far better than the one who sells in panic and waits
for "certainty" that never comes before the recovery.

### 4.3 Circle of competence
`tags: behavior, circle-of-competence, philosophy`
Invest only within your "circle of competence" — the set of businesses you genuinely
understand. The size of the circle matters far less than knowing where its edges are.
Buffett and Munger pass on opportunities they can't evaluate, even profitable-looking
ones, rather than pretend to understand them. For an investor, a domain you know deeply
from work or study (e.g. semiconductors) is a real edge, because you can judge that
industry's durability better than the average market participant.

## 5. LONG-TERM HOLDING & COMPOUNDING

### 5.1 Time and compounding
`tags: compounding, long-term, philosophy`
Buffett's favorite holding period is "forever." The power of compounding means that
returns build on prior returns, so the longest, most uninterrupted runway produces the
most wealth — interrupting compounding (by selling good businesses, or sitting in cash to
time the market) is costly. A great business held for decades lets the owner defer taxes
and let the engine run. Patience is not passive; it is the active discipline of doing
nothing when there is nothing worth doing.

### 5.2 Index funds for most people
`tags: index, VOO, humility, philosophy`
Despite being a famous stock-picker, Buffett's instruction for his own estate is to put
the vast majority in a very low-cost S&P 500 index fund. His view: most investors,
including most professionals, will do better in a cheap broad index than trying to pick
stocks, because fees and behavioral mistakes erode active returns. A low-cost index fund
is the humble default; concentrated stock-picking is only worthwhile if you have a real,
disciplined edge and the temperament to use it.
